# How our pricing works — in plain English

Everything on this page is live in the Founders Hub under **Pricing**. Numbers
come from the model, not from assumption.

---

## 1. The five things that eat a sale

Sell something for £30 and you do not have £30. Five things come out, and only
the first is obvious.

| | What it is | Roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The goods** | What PowerBody charge us | ~52% of their RRP |
| **Delivery** | What PowerBody charge to ship it | £6.50, £5.50, or free — see below |
| **VAT** | Nothing today. A fifth of every price once we register | 0% now |
| **Card fees** | Stripe's cut | 1.5% + 20p |
| **Returns** | 2% come back; we get the goods, never the postage | ~30p an order |

What's left after all five is **contribution** — the only number that means
anything. Everything in the hub measures margin against it.

---

## 2. Delivery is the thing that decides everything

PowerBody band delivery on **what we pay them for the order** — not on weight,
and not on what the customer pays us:

| Our wholesale on the order | They charge us |
| --- | --- |
| up to £50 | **£6.50** |
| up to £99 | **£5.50** |
| over £99 | **free** |

*(Highlands & Islands: £7.99, free over £300.)*

Three consequences, and they drive the whole catalogue strategy.

**Small orders are brutal.** £6.50 on a £22 monthly product is 30% of the
wholesale value gone before anything else. A single product on a monthly
subscription is close to unsellable at a sensible price.

**Big orders are free.** Clear £99 of wholesale in one shipment and delivery
costs nothing. That is worth more than any discount we could negotiate.

**So: ship the whole stack together, and ship it less often.** A three-product
stack sent quarterly can clear the free line in one parcel. The same three
products sent monthly, separately, pay delivery twelve times a year.

> ⚠️ The Dropshipping Guide quoted different rates — weight bands of £3.25–£5.17,
> and said free delivery was *not* available to dropshippers. The rates above are
> what the account actually shows. **Worth confirming with PowerBody**, because
> the small-order cost doubled and the large-order cost went to zero.

### Our free delivery is a different thing

We give the customer free delivery over £50 **of our retail prices**. PowerBody's
thresholds are on **their wholesale values**. Different numbers, different
bases — they are not related, and crossing them is how a margin model starts
believing postage is free.

Under our £50 line the customer's £3.95 roughly covers PowerBody's charge. Over
it we collect nothing and still pay them — on every qualifying order. That's the
cost of the promise, and it's a marketing cost, not a fulfilment one.

## 3. VAT — you're right, it largely cancels out

**Not registered (today):** we charge no VAT, so we keep the whole shelf price.
But we can't reclaim the VAT PowerBody charge us, so their £10 of stock really
costs £12 and their £6.50 delivery really costs £7.80.

**Registered (later):** we hand over a fifth of every sale, but reclaim theirs.

They *do* largely offset — but not exactly. Registering costs you **the VAT rate
times your margin**, not times your revenue:

```
registering costs ≈ 20% × (what you keep after paying for stock)
```

On a 40% margin that's about 8% of revenue. At current volumes the hub puts it
at roughly **£776 a year, or +8.7% on prices to stand still**.

The trap is that the VAT we're eating is a big visible number that makes
registering look like a saving. It isn't: you'd hand over more on sales than
you'd claim back on costs. Staying unregistered is right until turnover forces
it — the **VAT tab** tracks the £90,000 threshold and projects when that lands.

## 4. What the customer gives up and gets

**Three discounts, and they stack:**

1. **Subscribe & save** — 15% / 20% / 25% by bundle size
2. **First month** — a scratch card averaging 18% (top prize 50%, ~1 in 21)
3. **Free delivery** over £50

**Plus, when the partner programme goes live:** the influencer's code raises the
scratch card's floor to 25% — the card still runs and can still pay 50%, the
code just raises the worst outcome. It never stacks on top of a won card.

## 5. Where prices should come from — the open question

Here is the honest state of play, and it's the thing worth your attention.

**The model prices cost-plus:** add up the five costs, apply a target profit,
work backwards. That gives prices roughly **double PowerBody's own RRP** — £118
for a whey they say should retail at £64.99.

**That's the wrong frame for what we sell.** We're reselling Optimum Nutrition
and Applied Nutrition. Customers know what Gold Standard Whey costs; they can
check in ten seconds. Cost-plus works when you set the price — own-brand, or a
bundle nobody can price-match. It doesn't work for branded resale.

### Your anchoring point is the resolution

You said individual prices can sit higher because people buy through the quiz
and should feel they got a bargain. That's exactly right, and it turns the
"prices are too high" problem into a design:

- **Individual product price = the anchor.** Set it from RRP, not from cost —
  say **RRP + 15%**. It's the number people see next to the bundle.
- **The bundle is the bargain.** A 20% stack discount off RRP+15% lands the
  member at **~8% below RRP** — a real, checkable saving.
- **The model's job changes** from *"what should we charge?"* to *"at that
  price, do we still make money?"* — and it flags the products where we don't.

At +15% the anchor is high enough to make the discount feel real and low enough
that nobody catches us out. At +100% they'd catch us immediately.

### What that means product by product

Selling at RRP, some products work on subscription and some can't:

| | Margin |
| --- | --- |
| Gold Standard Whey 2.27kg *(lasts ~3 months)* | **+42%** |
| Micronised Creatine 634g *(lasts months)* | **+47%** |
| Beta-Alanine 500g | **+40%** |
| ISO-XP 1kg *(monthly)* | **−9%** |
| Diet Whey 1kg *(monthly)* | **−14%** |
| Pre-workouts *(monthly)* | **−6% to −9%** |

It's the same pattern as everything else on this page: **things that last two or
three months work, monthly things don't**, because one delivery gets spread
across the months instead of landing every month.

## 6. Influencer commission

**20% of net revenue on a member's first order, 10% on renewals for 12 months.**
Never on the gross — up to a fifth of that is HMRC's.

Blended over a six-month subscriber that's 11.3%. Across the whole book at 30%
attribution it's about **3.4% of net revenue** — the headline 20% is a per-order
rate, not a business-wide cost, and the two get confused constantly.

**Is 20% too high?** On products with room, no — Gold Standard still leaves
£10.84 after commission. On the thin ones it's unaffordable, but so is selling
them on subscription at all. Fix which products go on subscription and the
commission question mostly answers itself.

The hub's **"Are we making money?"** tab reports how far each lever could move
before the average order broke even. Partner share is one of them, and at current
settings it **cannot break it at any level** — 0% or 100% attributed, the average
stays positive. That's why not knowing the attribution rate isn't a risk.

## 7. Three decisions

**1. The bundle discount ladder.** You agreed 25% is too deep. Options:

| | Essentials | Performance | Complete |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Today | 15% | 20% | 25% |
| Option A | 15% | 18% | 20% |
| Option B | 10% | 15% | 20% |

Option B keeps the clearest ladder. I've left it at today's numbers — it changes
what every existing customer is quoted, so it's your call, not mine.

**2. Anchor from RRP rather than from cost.** Set list prices at RRP + ~15% and
turn the model into a profitability check. This is the change that fixes
"prices are too high" properly.

**3. Push subscriptions towards bigger, less frequent shipments.** Quarterly
delivery of a whole stack, aiming to clear PowerBody's £99 free-shipping line.
This is worth more than any pricing tweak on this page.
